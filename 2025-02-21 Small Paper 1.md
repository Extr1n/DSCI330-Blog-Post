## Theory: Connectionism
Connectionism is a cognitive science framework where cognition is formed from the interconnectedness of simple, small processing units. This theory was inspired by the structure of the brain, that is interconnected neurons to form a network of processing units. Connectionism is commonly used in AI through neural networks, where there are parallel smaller processing units that are connected together to come to the final conclusion. Philosophers have taken an interest in connectionism as it offers a different approach to cognition than the Theory of Mind, which is based on the idea that cognition is a single entity and similar to a computer. For the human brain, a naive approach to applying connectionism to the human brain is mapping each brain neuron to a specific function or object in memory. For example having a dog neuron that would activate when we think of a dog.

An example of a neural network is the following image:

![Neural Network](https://www.pngkey.com/png/full/876-8762622_our-technology-deep-learning-icon-png.png)

In the diagram depicted above, there are 2 starting nodes, each of which are given to the 4 middle nodes. The output of each of the middle nodes is then given to the final node which would then synthesize an output from the whole network. As such, connectionism is a theory that focuses on the interactions between interconnected processing units, rather than the whole brain as a single entity. With the correct terminology, the 2 starting nodes are called input units, while the 4 middle units are the hidden nodes and the final node is the output unit. While this diagram only depicts a single layer of hidden units, there can be multiple layers of hidden units within a single neural network. This can add a lot of complexity and computational power to the network. 

Before connectionalism had its time to shine as a prominent theory of cognition, many people believed in a more classical view which was that human cognition is analogous to symbolic use on computers. That is, humans store information as symbols (or strings of symbols) akin to that of storing data in a computer memory or writing symbols on a piece of paper (think of a Turing Machine). The classical view is that cognition stems from linear processing of these symbols, following a set of instructions. However connectionalism is quite different. Connectionalism emphasizes the idea of parallel processing and units having different "weights" or strengths. As such, connectionalism is a much more dynamic theory of cognition as a unit's activation is dependent on the activation of its neighbors. As such, connectionalism is a theory of cognition that is very different from the classical view of cognition.

That being said, some connectionists actually disagree that connecionalism rejects the classic view. These people are called implementational connectionists, who agree that the human mind is able to be a linear symbolic processor, but they recognize that the brain is an interconnected web of neurons working to accomplish this. As such, implementational connectionists aim to prove that linear symbolic processing can be achieved through neural networks. 

However, there exists radical connectionist that resist the notion of implementational connectionists. This is because these radical connectionists believe that linear symbolic processing is a poor way of representing how the human mind works. Thier argument stems from the belief that the human mind is a complex system that cannot be represented by a linear symbolic processor. As such, they argue that connectionism is a more natural way of representing the human mind.
