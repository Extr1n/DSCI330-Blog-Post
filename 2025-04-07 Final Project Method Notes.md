For my final project, I am investigating the relationship between various stressors (financial, social, academic, etc.) and mental health outcomes (depression, anxiety, and feelings of isolation) using survey data. My primary analytical method is multi-variable linear regression, which allows me to assess how each stressor contributes to mental health disorders while controlling for other factors.

According to William M. K. Trochim’s Research Methods Knowledge Base, linear regressions are used to show how independent variables can predict dependent variables. In the case of my research, the independent variables are the different pressures while the dependent variables are the different mental health disorders. Linear regressions assign coeffecients to each of the different independent variables (different external pressures) and the larger magnitude of a coeffecient indicates a stronger relationship between that independent variable and the dependent variable. 

The advantages of a multi-variable linear regression is it allows me to analyze multiple independent variables simultaneously while seeing which variables have a stronger correlation than others. This is important because it allows me to isolate each individual pressure while still keeping the whole picture in mind. It also allows me to easily draw insights of how much a single variable impacts each dependent variable. 

One critical flaw in these kinds of research is self-report bias (Althubaiti 2016), which essentially is that since individuals are reporting their own survey data it could be flawed. This is because oftentimes a person responds based on their own perspective as opposed to a perfectly normal distribution. However I have decided to lean into this notion of perspective because (as stated in my previous post), a person who is more affected by financial pressure will report a higher stress even if their financial pressure is numerically the same as another. This all has to do with percieved pressure, which is important to consider for these types of tools. However another flaw with this approach is the social desirability bias (Althubaiti 2016) which is the idea that people will answer the survey in a way that makes them appear socially normal. This is problematic because the survey alone would not be enough to differentiate someone who is lying about normality versus someone who is actually under little external pressure. That is why my idea with this tool is not to be used as something to diagnose patients, rather can be used as an indicator a patient could need a proper diagnosis.
