# Models: What are limitations with predictive models of decision making of individuals within groups?

There are often times where members of a given group need to act based on the actions of other individuals within the group. These kinds of scenarios are found in different games such as the price is right, split or steal, etc. While the decisions of individuals in a game can be modeled, no model can perfectly predict the decisions of a given individual.

First, a few awful approaches to modeling decision making would be randomness or even distributions. This is because any given individual would be trying to maximize their own earnings. For example, let's create a game where we have 100 individuals that can each pick any number 1-100 and the winner (or winners in case of a tie) is the individual(s) who guesses the number closest to 2/3 of the mean without exceeding this value. As such, if you assume everyone chooses randomly then the optimal choice is clearly to pick the number 33 or 32. However, in the case where you are actually a participant in this game, you are very unlikely to win because all other participants would follow this logic and select 32 or 33. In which case the individual closest to 22 would win (if all people choose 32/33 then no one would win). We can use this to create a new model of assuming every individual is perfectly logical. 

Assuming everyone is perfectly logical in this game, then everyone would realize the winning number would be 32/33, which then makes the winning number 22, which then makes the number 14, so on and so forth. Eventually after a few cycles everyone would come to the conclusion that everyone will pick 1 and no one will win. Now, if this game were to happen in actuality would you pick 1? If you would pick 1, you probably would not win because you assume the mean of this arbitrary group would be around 2-3. However, the mean would probably not be 2-3 because we as humans are subject to Bounded Rationality. 

Bounded rationality is a model of decision making by Herbert Simon which asserts that people often lack sufficient information, time, or other resources to make a fully rational decision. As such, people are often content with making a good enough decision as opposed to the optimal decision from a perfectly rational logician. While this model appears sufficient at a surface level, there is a very critical flaw. What is a person’s good enough decision? Given the same number game, a given individual could pick 33, 10, 20, who knows? Assuming each participant is allowed as much time as they want to answer, they are solely bound by the fact they do not know what other participants picked and of course, their own cognitive limit. This means an individual's bounds widely vary based on one’s cognitive capacity and overall interest in the activity. Bounded rationality lacks a deterministic predictiveness of the outcome, it simply states that individuals are unlikely to make the most optimal decision.

Any model will lack the capability of predicting the complexities of emotional, societal, or cultural influence. Many frameworks used in these kinds of models assume a universal set of values and rationality that ignore many emotions or norms that drive decision making in the real world. For example, in the real world, emotions drive people toward decisions even if they aren’t rational. Emotions can be the reason a business would keep a loyal employee despite their poor overall performance in the company. Additionally, differences in values is what drives our voting system today. People vote on different topics in order to implement a system that correlates with their own personal beliefs or values. However even a majority rules approach isn’t applicable across all cultures. While America uses a majority rules approach, Japan focuses on a concept called nemawashi which involves gathering feedback from those who are opposed to a proposed change in order to get a group consensus before making a decision. There are a wide variety of nuances between different cultures, backgrounds, values, beliefs, etc that cannot be summed up into a single simplistic predictive model of decision making.
